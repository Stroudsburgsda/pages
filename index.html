Prophetic & Legal Analysis

When Government Promotes Religion: Should Christians Be Concerned?

Public Schools, Church-State Separation, and Freedom of Conscience

At first glance, this may sound like good news for Christians: The Ten Commandments are returning to public-school classrooms.

Texas enacted a law requiring public schools to display the Ten Commandments when qualifying displays are donated. Similar efforts have emerged in other states. Now the controversy could be heading to the United States Supreme Court, which was asked this week to review the Texas law.

As Bible-believing Christians, shouldn’t we celebrate? Perhaps the better question is: What happens when government begins deciding how religion should be promoted?

A Very Adventist Concern

Seventh-day Adventists strongly believe in the Ten Commandments. We believe the seventh-day Sabbath remains part of God’s moral law:

“Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy.” — Exodus 20:8

But Adventists have historically defended another biblical principle just as strongly: Government should protect religion without controlling, establishing, or enforcing religion.

That distinction matters. Today government may promote something with which we agree. Tomorrow government may promote a religious practice with which we do not agree.

Think About the Fourth Commandment

Here is an uncomfortable question for Sabbath keepers:

What if government becomes increasingly comfortable promoting Christianity, but the Christianity being promoted eventually identifies Sunday, rather than the seventh-day Sabbath, as the nation’s sacred day?

Would we still want government determining religious observance?

This is why Adventists should be careful not to measure religious-liberty developments simply by asking: “Is Christianity winning?” The better question is: “Is freedom of conscience being protected?”

Our Own Church Is Speaking About This

The Seventh-day Adventist Church in North America recently addressed this very issue before the federal Religious Liberty Commission.

The church reaffirmed its historic position that government should vigorously protect the free exercise of religion while remaining neutral in matters of faith.

That is an important distinction. Religious liberty does not require a government hostile toward Christianity. Neither does it require a government that promotes Christianity. It requires a government that protects the Christian, the Jew, the Muslim, the atheist, and the Seventh-day Adventist from being forced in matters of conscience.

Revelation 13 Should Make Us Think

For Adventists, this discussion goes beyond American constitutional law. Revelation 13 describes a remarkable future union involving religion, political authority, worship, and coercion.

The warning is not that society will become completely irreligious. The warning is that religion itself will eventually receive the power of the state to enforce worship. That distinction is crucial. The final crisis is fundamentally a crisis over worship and conscience.

“And causeth the earth and them which dwell therein to worship…” — Revelation 13:12

“And that no man might buy or sell…” — Revelation 13:17

The Progression Should Command Our Attention:

1. Religion 2. Government Authority 3. Worship 4. Economic Pressure 5. Coercion

Responsible Prophetic Understanding

We must be responsible students of prophecy. A law displaying the Ten Commandments in a classroom is not the mark of the beast, nor should every church-state controversy be labeled a direct fulfillment of Revelation 13.

But developments like these should cause Adventists to think seriously about a principle: Should government have the power to advance religious belief in the first place?

Because once that principle is accepted, the question eventually becomes: Which religion? Which interpretation? Which commandment? Which day of worship?

Religious Liberty Watch

This week, don’t merely watch whether Christianity gains greater influence in government. Watch the relationship between religious institutions and government power.

  • There is a difference between protecting Christians from persecution and giving Christianity governmental authority.
  • There is a difference between allowing prayer and prescribing prayer.
  • There is a difference between protecting Sabbath observance and government determining a day of worship.
  • And there is a profound difference between religious freedom and religious power.

This Week's Religious Liberty Thought

"As Seventh-day Adventists, we should never fear genuine religious freedom. But neither should we become so excited when government favors Christianity that we forget our prophetic message about what can happen when church and state join hands."

"Today, government may support a religious idea we cherish. Tomorrow, the same power may be used to enforce one we cannot obey. The safest government for the church is not one that controls religion, but one that protects every person’s freedom to choose whom and how they will worship."

“Choose you this day whom ye will serve.” — Joshua 24:15

1 Corinthians 13 Study | Sabbath 8/15–8/22/2026

A Portrait Worth Studying

This week's lesson, “A Portrait of Love,” takes us to one of the most familiar passages in 1 Corinthians. We’re exploring 1 Corinthians 13. Paul is not simply giving us a beautiful description of love. He is showing us what the Christian life should look like when Christ’s love is at work in us. The lesson reminds us that love is not merely an emotion or something we say; it is an attitude expressed through our actions, words, and choices.

It is easy to read, “Love is patient, love is kind,” and think of someone else who needs to become more loving. But Paul's words were written to a church struggling with pride, division, competition, and self-interest. His portrait of love was not theoretical—it was a direct challenge to how believers treated one another.

Look closely at Paul's language. He not only tells us what love does: it is patient, it is kind, it rejoices in truth, it believes, hopes, and endures. But he also tells us what love does not: love does not envy, boast, become arrogant, seek its own way, keep a record of wrongs, or delight in another person's failure. Love is revealed not simply by what we feel, but by how we behave when relationships become difficult.

Paul's portrait of love is ultimately a portrait of Jesus. Christ perfectly demonstrated the patience, kindness, humility, forgiveness, endurance, and self-giving love described in 1 Corinthians 13. When we study this chapter, we are not merely learning how to become "nicer" people. We are looking at the character of Christ and asking the Holy Spirit to reproduce that character in us.

That makes this week's lesson especially important for our church. Spiritual gifts, knowledge, faith, generosity, and even sacrifice can accomplish much, but without love, they lose their true significance. Love is not one quality among many; it is the atmosphere in which everything else in the Christian life must operate.

Food For Thought

"If 1 Corinthians 13 is a portrait of Jesus, and your life is the canvas, what aspect of Christ's love is the Holy Spirit asking you to paint more clearly this week?"
