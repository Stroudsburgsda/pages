Midweek Momentum: The Gift of Prophecy

Sabbath School Lesson Study Preview for Sabbath, October 17, 2026

Old Testament Prophets

Have you ever been so discouraged by the results of your efforts that you began to wonder whether anything you did really mattered?

This week, we meet Elijah, a prophet who witnessed God's extraordinary power on Mount Carmel, yet later found himself exhausted, afraid, and ready to give up. How could someone who had experienced so much of God's power reach such a low point?

Elijah's story reminds us that God’s servants can experience human weakness without God abandoning them. God understands the humanness/humanity of His instruments.

That’s only part of this week's lesson! What made Micaiah willing to stand alone when hundreds of other prophets told the king what he wanted to hear? Why did King Josiah's officials seek out Huldah for spiritual guidance? And what can the experiences of these prophets teach us about recognizing God's truth and responding to His call?

Your Midweek Challenge

Set aside some time to study the lesson, explore the Bible passages for the remaining days, and reflect on this question:

“Am I willing to hear God's truth even when it challenges my expectations, exposes my weaknesses, or calls me out of my comfort zone?”

Let's come to Sabbath School prepared not just to recount what the prophets did, but to share what their experiences reveal about God and what He desires to do in our lives.

Don’t just read the lesson; let the lesson read you and change you.

Religious Liberty: Then & Now

The Sunday Law That Went to Congress: When an Adventist Leader Challenged the United States Government

Imagine waking up one morning to discover that Congress was considering a law that could affect how you observe your faith.

Not a local ordinance. Not a workplace policy. A national law establishing Sunday as a day of rest.

For Seventh-day Adventists in 1888, this was not a hypothetical situation. It was happening. And one Adventist leader was determined to speak out.

The Blair Sunday-Rest Bill of 1888

In May 1888, Senator Henry W. Blair of New Hampshire introduced Senate Bill 2983, commonly known as the Blair Sunday-Rest Bill. The legislation sought to secure Sunday rest by restricting certain forms of labor, commerce, transportation, and recreation under federal jurisdiction.

Supporters argued that the nation needed a common day of rest to protect workers, families, and society. To many Americans, the proposal sounded reasonable. After all, what could be wrong with giving people a day to rest?

But Seventh-day Adventists recognized a serious problem. Why should the government determine which day Americans should observe? The proposed legislation favored Sunday, the traditional Christian day of worship, raising serious concerns for those who observed a different day.

One Adventist Stood Before Congress

On December 13, 1888, Alonzo T. Jones, a Seventh-day Adventist minister, appeared before the United States Senate Committee on Education and Labor to oppose the proposed legislation. Jones was not a senator, lawyer, or powerful political figure. He was a minister representing a relatively small religious community. But his message challenged the very foundation of the proposed law.

Jones argued that government had no rightful authority to enforce religious observance, even when the majority supported it. His position was significant because he did not simply ask Congress to exempt Seventh-day Adventists. He challenged the government’s authority to legislate religious observance in the first place.

The Danger of a Well-Intentioned Law

Here is what makes this historical event so important. The proposed Sunday legislation was presented, in part, as a measure to promote rest and protect society. Those were goals many Americans could appreciate. But Adventists understood that even a law presented as beneficial could threaten religious freedom if it gave government authority over religious practice.

Consider the question: If government can establish Sunday observance because most Christians support it, what prevents government from enforcing other religious practices in the future? And what happens to the minority whose convictions differ?

For Adventists, the issue was never whether Sunday worshipers had the right to observe Sunday. They absolutely did. The issue was whether that observance should be enforced through civil law.

What Happened to the Bill?

The Blair Sunday-Rest Bill did not become law. But the controversy was far from over. Sunday laws continued to exist in several states, and Sabbath-keeping Adventists continued facing legal difficulties.

The national debate revealed something that our pioneers understood clearly: Religious freedom can be threatened even when those proposing restrictions believe they are doing something good. That is why religious liberty requires more than good intentions. It requires clear limits on government authority.

Why This Matters Today

More than 130 years have passed since A. T. Jones appeared before Congress. Yet the questions raised in 1888 remain relevant. When religious organizations seek government support, where should the boundaries be drawn? When lawmakers promote policies rooted in religious traditions, how do we ensure that minority faiths remain protected? And perhaps most importantly: Would we recognize a threat to religious liberty if the proposed law supported something we personally believed in?

As Seventh-day Adventists, we must remember that protecting religious freedom is not the same as opposing Christianity in public life. Christians have every right to participate in public discussions, advocate for their beliefs, and practice their faith. But there is an important difference between persuading someone to embrace a religious belief and using government authority to require religious observance.

Jesus never commissioned His followers to compel worship through civil power. He invited people to follow Him:

“If any man will come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me.” — Matthew 16:24

The invitation begins with a choice.

From the Adventist Archives: Did You Know?

A. T. Jones’s 1888 testimony before the Senate committee became an important part of early Adventist religious-liberty literature. His arguments were published in a work titled The National Sunday Law. The publication preserved the Adventist opposition to government-enforced religious observance and helped educate church members about the importance of religious freedom.

The Blair Bill ultimately failed, but the experience strengthened the growing Adventist religious-liberty movement. Our pioneers understood that freedom of conscience was worth defending before it was lost.

Religious Liberty Thought for the Week:
A law does not become harmless simply because its supporters have good intentions. When government begins determining religious observance, freedom of conscience is placed at risk. Our Adventist pioneers understood this principle more than a century ago. The question is whether we understand it just as clearly today.

“Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar’s; and unto God the things that are God’s.” — Matthew 22:21
