Midweek Momentum: The Gift of Prophecy

Lesson Study: The Gift of Prophecy — Revelation and Inspiration

As we continue to study this quarter’s lesson on prophecy, I hope you see God’s desire to connect with humanity. This week, as we explore Revelation and Inspiration, we learn not simply how the Bible came to us, but how God used human messengers to communicate His truth.

As you study, look for these powerful insights:

  • God wants us to know Him. Why has He chosen to communicate with humanity in so many different ways?
  • God's message, human messengers. How did the Holy Spirit work through people with their own personalities, experiences, and writing styles?
  • God's message was meant to endure. Why was it important for prophetic messages to be written down?
  • God knows how to get our attention. What can we learn from the dramatic and symbolic ways He sometimes communicated?

Weekly Personal Reflection

Have you ever struggled to understand something in the Bible? How does knowing more about revelation and inspiration shape your confidence in God's Word?

“While God used human messengers, His message points us to divine truth.”

Come prepared to participate! Bring a Scripture that stood out to you, a question you've wrestled with, or an insight that strengthened your faith. Let's learn from one another as we explore how God has communicated His truth.

Don't wait until Sabbath to open your Bible. Study throughout the week, invite the Holy Spirit to guide your understanding, and come ready to share! Let's move beyond simply reading the Word to understanding why we can trust the God who gave it to us.

Religious Liberty: Then & Now

Could It Happen Again? The Lessons of Yesterday and the Warnings of Today

In the late 1800s, Seventh-day Adventists were arrested, fined, and imprisoned for working on Sunday. In 1888, Senator Henry W. Blair introduced legislation seeking to establish Sunday rest under federal law.

Our Adventist pioneers recognized the danger. They organized, spoke before Congress, published religious liberty literature, and challenged the idea that government should determine religious observance.

More than 130 years later, we must ask: Could something similar happen again? And perhaps the more important question is: Would we recognize the warning signs?

America in 2026: A Changing Religious Landscape

Across America, discussions about the relationship between Christianity and government have intensified. In recent years, several states have advanced legislation concerning religious displays and religious instruction in public schools. Louisiana and Texas, for example, enacted laws requiring displays of the Ten Commandments in public-school classrooms, prompting legal challenges over the constitutional separation of church and state.

Meanwhile, debates about Christian nationalism, religious influence in public policy, and the government’s responsibility to protect religious expression continue to shape the national conversation. Some Christians welcome these developments as a restoration of America’s religious heritage. Others, including religious liberty advocates, question whether increased governmental involvement in religion could eventually threaten freedom of conscience.

The concern is not that Christians are participating in government. The concern arises when government begins using its authority to promote or enforce religious observance. That distinction is essential.

The Parallel Our Pioneers Would Recognize

Consider what happened in 1888. Supporters of Sunday legislation argued that a common day of rest would benefit society, families, and workers. Those goals sounded reasonable. But Adventists understood that when government establishes a religiously significant day as a legal requirement, people whose convictions differ may face serious consequences.

Today, conversations about religion and government often begin with similarly appealing objectives: strengthening families, restoring morality, preserving traditions, and promoting national unity. These goals are not inherently wrong. But history teaches us to ask: At what point does promoting religious values become enforcing religious practice? And who decides which religious beliefs receive government support?

What About Sunday Legislation Today?

There is an important distinction we must make. America does not currently have a federal law requiring Sunday worship. Some states continue to maintain Sunday-related restrictions, often called blue laws, governing certain commercial activities. Such laws have historically been justified for both religious and secular reasons. A Sunday closing law is not automatically a law compelling worship.

Nevertheless, Adventist history reminds us why legislation involving religiously significant days deserves careful examination. The critical issue is whether government protects a person’s freedom to choose or uses civil authority to compel religious observance.

Ellen White’s Warning

“The dignitaries of church and state will unite to bribe, persuade, or compel all classes to honor the Sunday.”
— The Great Controversy, p. 592

For Seventh-day Adventists, this warning has long been connected to our understanding of Revelation 13, which describes a future crisis involving worship, civil authority, and economic pressure (Revelation 13:17). Revelation 14:12 then identifies God’s faithful people: “Here is the patience of the saints: here are they that keep the commandments of God, and the faith of Jesus.”

The issue is not merely a day. It is worship, obedience, and allegiance to God. But we must be careful not to declare every religious law, political movement, or court decision a fulfillment of prophecy. Prophecy calls us to be watchful, not reckless in our conclusions.

History Also Teaches Us Something Hopeful

There is another lesson from the 1880s that we should not overlook: The Blair Sunday-Rest Bill failed. Our pioneers did not simply predict trouble and wait for it to happen. They acted. They educated their communities, wrote articles, challenged legislation, appealed to lawmakers, defended the rights of religious minorities, and helped establish a religious liberty ministry that continues today. Their example reminds us that Christians can be both prophetically aware and actively engaged in protecting freedom.

What Is Our Responsibility Today?

Religious liberty is not merely the responsibility of pastors, lawyers, or church administrators. Every church member has a role. We can educate ourselves about religious freedom, follow legislative developments, communicate respectfully with elected officials, and defend the rights of those whose beliefs differ from our own. We can also remember that religious liberty begins with how we treat one another: Do we respect another person’s conscience? Do we defend religious freedom only when Adventists are affected? Are we willing to speak when someone’s freedom is threatened, even when we disagree with their beliefs?

From the Adventist Archives: Did You Know?

The Seventh-day Adventist Church’s organized religious liberty work traces its roots to the National Religious Liberty Association, established in 1889. What began during an era of Sunday-law prosecutions developed into an international ministry advocating religious freedom. Today, the church continues that work through its Public Affairs and Religious Liberty ministry and the International Religious Liberty Association. The mission remains rooted in the same principle: Religious freedom must be protected for everyone, not merely for those who share our faith.

Religious Liberty Thought for the Week:
The greatest danger is not simply that history might repeat itself. It is that we might forget the lessons history has already taught us. Our pioneers recognized the danger of allowing civil authority to enforce religious observance. They refused to remain silent. They defended their convictions while advocating freedom for others.

“Watch ye, stand fast in the faith, quit you like men, be strong.” — 1 Corinthians 16:13

Religious liberty is not merely an Adventist heritage. It is an Adventist responsibility.
