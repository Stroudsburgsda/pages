Sabbath School Lesson Preview

Stewardship & Mission

Sabbath 9/12/2026

"When we think about stewardship, we often think about what we give. However, this week’s lesson asks us to think about who we are becoming."

Paul’s message to the Corinthians begins with an unexpected example: people experiencing extreme poverty who were overflowing with generosity. Their generosity wasn't driven by abundance, pressure, or obligation. It flowed from God's grace.

“For ye know the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ, that, though He was rich, yet for your sakes He became poor, that ye through His poverty might be rich.” — 2 Corinthians 8:9

Jesus didn't simply give something for the mission. Jesus gave Himself. That's the heart of stewardship.

Reframing the Question

As you study 2 Corinthians 8–9 this week, don't just ask: “How much should I give?”
Ask instead: “What does God's grace motivate me to give?”

Because stewardship isn't limited to our wallets. The Macedonians “gave themselves first to the Lord.” And their giving flowed outward from that surrender.

Before Sabbath, Ask Yourself:

Where is God inviting me to become more generous—not just with my money, but with myself?

Stewardship and mission are inseparable. What God places in our hands is ultimately intended to participate in His work of blessing others.

Religious Liberty: Then & Now

Series: Week 1 Adventist Heritage & Conscience

When Keeping the Sabbath Could Land You in Jail

Tennessee 1889 • Robert M. King • Conscience vs. Sunday Laws

"Imagine this: You faithfully keep the seventh-day Sabbath. Friday evening comes. You put your work aside. You worship, rest, and honor the Sabbath according to the fourth commandment. Sunday morning comes, and you return to work. Then you are arrested."

It sounds almost unbelievable today. But for Seventh-day Adventists in nineteenth-century America, it happened.

Meet Robert M. King

Robert M. King was a Seventh-day Adventist farmer living in rural Tennessee. King believed what Adventists still believe today: “The seventh day is the sabbath of the LORD thy God.” (Exodus 20:10). So he rested on Saturday.

But King was also a poor farmer with a family to support. He could not afford to stop working both Saturday and Sunday every week. On Sunday, June 23, 1889, King quietly went into his cornfield to work. He wasn't holding a protest. He wasn't disrupting a Sunday church service. He wasn't trying to prevent anyone else from worshiping. In fact, his field was secluded.

But someone was watching. A local Sunday-school superintendent went out to King's property and waited until King became visible between the rows of corn. King was reported and arrested.

His crime? Working on Sunday.

His "Crime" & The Chain Gangs

King was initially fined under Tennessee's Sunday law. But the controversy didn't stop there. He continued working on Sundays, and prosecutors eventually charged his Sunday farm work as a “common nuisance.”

At trial, something remarkable became clear: the witnesses against King acknowledged that his work had not physically disturbed their Sunday worship. What bothered them was that he was working on the day they considered sacred. King was convicted and eventually fined $75 plus costs—a substantial amount for a poor farmer in the 1890s. His case moved through the courts as Adventists fought on his behalf.

And King Wasn't Alone:

In Tennessee, other Seventh-day Adventists were arrested for ordinary Sunday activities such as farming, plowing, hoeing, and cutting wood. Some were jailed. Some were placed in chain gangs. In 1892, three Adventist men who had already spent 44 days in jail were marched through the streets of Paris, Tennessee, and put to work shoveling on the public highway.

Think about the irony: They rested on the seventh day because God commanded it—and were punished because they would not also treat Sunday as sacred.

Why Does This History Matter?

It would be easy to read this story and simply say: “That was more than 130 years ago.” But there is a larger Religious Liberty lesson here. The issue wasn't whether Sunday-keeping Christians had the right to worship on Sunday. Of course they did. The issue was whether their religious observance should become a rule that everyone else was legally required to follow.

That distinction is at the heart of religious liberty. Robert King wasn't demanding that his neighbors keep Saturday. He simply wanted the freedom to keep Saturday himself without being forced by law to observe another religious community's day of rest.

Here Is the Question for Us

Suppose the situation were reversed. Imagine living somewhere that legally required businesses to close and citizens to refrain from ordinary work on Friday because the majority considered Friday sacred. Or suppose government required everyone to observe a religious practice belonging to another faith. We would immediately recognize the problem.

So why should the principle change when the government-mandated practice happens to be Christian? Religious liberty isn't the freedom to impose our religion. It is the freedom to practice our faith while allowing our neighbor that same freedom of conscience.

From the Adventist Archives

Robert M. King's case eventually reached federal court. The official record described him as a Seventh-day Adventist who “conscientiously and very strictly observed” the seventh day and worked on Sunday because, as a poor man, he could not afford to surrender two working days every week.

King's case became one of the early battles that helped shape the Adventist Church's commitment to defending religious liberty.

“We ought to obey God rather than men.” — Acts 5:29
