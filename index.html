Lesson Study Momentum

1 Corinthians 13 Study | Sabbath 8/15/2026 Preview

A Portrait Worth Studying

This week's lesson, “A Portrait of Love,” takes us to one of the most familiar passages in 1 Corinthians. We’re exploring 1 Corinthians 13. Paul is not simply giving us a beautiful description of love. He is showing us what the Christian life should look like when Christ’s love is at work in us. The lesson reminds us that love is not merely an emotion or something we say; it is an attitude expressed through our actions, words, and choices.

It is easy to read, “Love is patient, love is kind,” and think of someone else who needs to become more loving. But Paul's words were written to a church struggling with pride, division, competition, and self-interest. His portrait of love was not theoretical—it was a direct challenge to how believers treated one another.

Look closely at Paul's language. He not only tells us what love does: it is patient, it is kind, it rejoices in truth, it believes, hopes, and endures. But he also tells us what love does not: love does not envy, boast, become arrogant, seek its own way, keep a record of wrongs, or delight in another person's failure. Love is revealed not simply by what we feel, but by how we behave when relationships become difficult.

Paul's portrait of love is ultimately a portrait of Jesus. Christ perfectly demonstrated the patience, kindness, humility, forgiveness, endurance, and self-giving love described in 1 Corinthians 13. When we study this chapter, we are not merely learning how to become "nicer" people. We are looking at the character of Christ and asking the Holy Spirit to reproduce that character in us.

That makes this week's lesson especially important for our church. Spiritual gifts, knowledge, faith, generosity, and even sacrifice can accomplish much, but without love, they lose their true significance. Love is not one quality among many; it is the atmosphere in which everything else in the Christian life must operate. And unlike the gifts that will one day fulfill their purpose, love will never pass away.

So this week, don't simply read 1 Corinthians 13. Study it slowly. Pray through it. Put your name beside each characteristic and ask where Christ is inviting you to grow. Perhaps the Holy Spirit is calling you to become more patient with someone, more generous with forgiveness, less concerned with being right, or more willing to love when there is nothing to gain in return.

Food For Thought

"If 1 Corinthians 13 is a portrait of Jesus, and your life is the canvas, what aspect of Christ's love is the Holy Spirit asking you to paint more clearly this week?"

Religious Liberty Corner

Religious Liberty Feature

When Faith & Your Job Collide: Know Where You Stand

Freedom of Conscience & Workplace Accommodations

Could your religious convictions cost you your job? For many believers, that is not a hypothetical question.

Just this month, a Michigan healthcare system agreed to a $410,000 settlement in a religious-discrimination lawsuit brought by a Christian physician assistant who said she was terminated after requesting a religious accommodation concerning workplace requirements that conflicted with her beliefs. As part of the settlement, the healthcare system also agreed to implement a religious-accommodation policy and provide training regarding religious accommodation.

Whatever one thinks about the particular issue involved in that case, the religious-liberty principle should concern every Seventh-day Adventist: What happens when the requirements of an employer conflict with a sincerely held religious conviction? For Adventists, that question has special significance because we already know one primary area in which conflict can arise: the seventh-day Sabbath.

The Law Has Changed in an Important Way

In 2023, the United States Supreme Court issued an important decision in Groff v. DeJoy, a case involving a Christian postal worker who sought accommodation from Sunday work. For decades, employers had often relied upon a relatively low standard for claiming that accommodating an employee’s religion created an “undue hardship.”

The Supreme Court rejected that interpretation. Under the clarified standard, an employer must show that the burden of an accommodation would be substantial in the overall context of the employer’s business. That matters. Federal law generally requires covered employers to reasonably accommodate sincerely held religious practices unless the accommodation would create such an undue hardship. Possible accommodations can include scheduling changes, voluntary shift swaps, reassignment, or adjustments to workplace policies. For a Seventh-day Adventist requesting Sabbath accommodation, this strengthened standard can be extremely important.

But There Is a Larger Issue

Religious liberty is not merely about winning court cases. It is about conscience. Daniel was told not to pray. The three Hebrew young men were commanded to worship. The apostles were ordered not to preach. In each case, human authority eventually crossed a boundary that belonged to God. Their response was not rebellion for rebellion’s sake. It was faithful obedience:

“We ought to obey God rather than men.” — Acts 5:29

And Bible prophecy tells us that the question of conscience will become even more serious. Revelation 13 describes a time when religious observance, government authority, and economic pressure become intertwined, eventually reaching the point where people cannot “buy or sell” without submitting to the imposed system. For Seventh-day Adventists, therefore, religious liberty is not a side issue. It is part of our prophetic identity.

Why Should We Defend Someone Who Believes Differently?

Because religious liberty cannot mean freedom only for people who believe as we do. Today the controversy may involve another Christian’s conviction. Tomorrow it may involve a Muslim’s prayer practice, a Jewish believer’s holy day, or someone else’s sincere religious observance.

And one day the question may be: Will you work on the seventh-day Sabbath? If we want our conscience protected when that day comes, we must defend the principle of freedom of conscience now, even when the person asking for protection believes differently than we do.

Religious Liberty Watch

Pay attention when you hear words such as: “religious accommodation,” “freedom of conscience,” “compelled religious activity,” “undue hardship,” and “church and state.”

These are not merely legal phrases. They concern a question that will become increasingly important as we approach the final events described in Scripture: Who has authority over the conscience? Government has legitimate authority. Employers have legitimate authority. But neither has unlimited authority over a person’s relationship with God.

“Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar’s; and unto God the things that are God’s.” — Matthew 22:21

This Week's Religious Liberty Thought

"Do not wait until your Sabbath is threatened to become interested in religious liberty. Learn the principles now. Know your rights now. Respect the conscience of others now. And most importantly, develop the kind of relationship with Christ that will enable you to stand faithfully when obedience becomes costly."

Religious liberty protects the freedom to choose. Prophecy reminds us that one day that choice may come with a price.
