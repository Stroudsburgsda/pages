Midweek Momentum: The Gift of Prophecy

Sabbath School Lesson Study Preview for Sabbath, October 24, 2026

New Testament Prophets

Happy Wednesday, Sabbath School!

Have you ever wondered how we can recognize God’s voice amid all the voices competing for our attention? This week’s lesson, New Testament Prophets, reminds us that while times and circumstances may change, the Holy Spirit remains the source of true prophecy.

As you study this week, consider these three important truths:

Jesus Is the Center

All true prophetic messages point us to Christ and deepen our understanding of His love and plan of salvation.

Spiritual Gifts Have a Purpose

Prophecy is not merely about revealing what is to come. It also strengthens, encourages, and comforts God’s people. How can we use our own spiritual gifts to build others up?

Discernment Is Essential

Not every message that appears spiritual comes from God. We must test what we hear against His Word and keep Jesus at the center of our faith.

Let’s Make This Sabbath School Lesson a Conversation, Not Just a Reading Assignment!

Here’s your midweek challenge: Set aside time to revisit the daily readings, reflect on the questions, and come to Sabbath School ready to contribute. Choose one insight, one question, or one personal application from this week’s lesson that you can share with the class. Perhaps a Scripture stood out to you, a truth challenged your thinking, or a question made you look more closely at God’s Word. Bring it with you!

And remember, participation isn't reserved for those who have all the answers. Sometimes the most meaningful discussions begin with an honest question or a perspective that helps someone else see Scripture in a new way. Your voice matters, your insights are valuable, and your participation can be a blessing to the entire class.

Coming Prepared

Let’s come to Sabbath School prepared not simply to listen, but to engage, encourage one another, and grow together in faith. Our class is richer when every member comes ready to participate!

See you on Sabbath, family! ❤️

Religious Liberty: Then & Now

When Adventists Became Defenders of Everyone’s Freedom: The Movement That Began With a Few Believers and Reached the World

Imagine being arrested for practicing your faith, fighting for your rights, and then deciding that protecting your own religious freedom wasn’t enough. You would fight for the religious freedom of people who didn’t even believe what you believed.

That is exactly the direction our Adventist pioneers took. Last week, we learned how A. T. Jones stood before Congress in 1888 to oppose a proposed national Sunday law. But the story did not end when that legislation failed. Something much bigger was beginning.

From Persecution to Organization

By the late 1880s, Seventh-day Adventists had experienced the consequences of religious legislation firsthand. Sabbath keepers had been arrested, fined, and imprisoned under Sunday laws. At the same time, religious organizations were pushing for greater government recognition of Christian practices.

Our pioneers realized that simply defending individual Adventists in court was not enough. They needed to educate the public, influence lawmakers, and defend the constitutional principles that protected freedom of conscience.

So, on July 21, 1889, the National Religious Liberty Association was organized with just 110 members. Think about that: a relatively small denomination, still establishing itself in America, decided to take on a national issue involving religion, government, and individual freedom. And its mission would eventually reach far beyond the United States.

The Power of the Printed Word

Even before the association was formally organized, Adventists recognized the importance of educating the public. In January 1886, they began publishing a periodical called The American Sentinel. Its purpose was to defend religious liberty and oppose the improper union of church and state. The publication challenged Sunday legislation, addressed constitutional questions, and presented arguments for protecting religious minorities.

This was not simply a church newsletter. It was an effort to influence the national conversation about religious freedom. Our pioneers understood something that remains true today: if people do not understand their freedoms, they may not recognize when those freedoms are being threatened.

Defending the Freedom of All

The early Adventists faced a difficult question: Should they defend only Sabbath keepers? Or should they defend the religious freedom of people whose beliefs they considered incorrect?

Their developing answer was significant: Religious liberty must belong to everyone, or it is not truly religious liberty.

One example came during the 1890s, when anti-Catholic movements sought government policies that would restrict the freedom of Catholic families to educate their children according to their religious convictions. Adventist religious liberty advocates, including American Sentinel editor Calvin P. Bollman, challenged such efforts.

Consider the significance. Adventists strongly disagreed with important teachings of the Roman Catholic Church. Yet they recognized that government interference with Catholic religious freedom threatened the very principle that protected Adventists. They understood that defending someone’s religious liberty did not mean endorsing that person’s theology. It meant defending the freedom to follow conscience without improper government interference. That is a lesson the church must never forget.

From a National Movement to an International Mission

In 1893, the movement expanded into the International Religious Liberty Association (IRLA). That same year, Adventist religious liberty advocates adopted a declaration of principles affirming both respect for legitimate civil government and opposition to government legislation on religious questions.

Their position was clear: Civil government has an important role in maintaining order and protecting society. But government must not assume authority over religious conscience.

The movement continued to grow. In 1906, the church began publishing Liberty magazine, carrying forward the work of educating readers about religious freedom. What began as a response to Sunday-law persecution developed into an enduring ministry advocating freedom of conscience.

What Would Our Pioneers Think of Us Today?

Our pioneers faced arrests, imprisonment, and public opposition. Yet they organized, published, educated, and advocated. They did not remain silent simply because the issue was controversial.

Today, we have access to information, legal resources, public officials, and opportunities to speak about religious freedom that many early Adventists could scarcely have imagined. But how much do we actually know about religious liberty? Do we understand the rights we enjoy? Do we pay attention when those rights are challenged? And are we willing to defend religious freedom when the person affected belongs to another faith?

Religious liberty cannot become important to us only when Adventists are affected.

“Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them.” — Matthew 7:12

We want others to respect our right to worship on the seventh-day Sabbath. We want employers to respect our sincerely held religious convictions. We want government to protect our freedom to practice our faith. But Christ calls us to extend that same respect to others. We cannot demand freedom for ourselves while remaining indifferent when someone else’s conscience is threatened.

From the Adventist Archives: Did You Know?

The International Religious Liberty Association traces its origins to the National Religious Liberty Association established in 1889. In 1893, its declaration of principles affirmed:

“We believe it is right, and should be the privilege of every man to worship according to the dictates of his own conscience.”

That principle became central to the Adventist approach to religious liberty. Our pioneers recognized that freedom of conscience was not a privilege reserved for one denomination. It was a right that should be protected for all.

Religious Liberty Thought for the Week:
Our pioneers did not fight for religious freedom simply so Adventists could be free. They fought because they believed freedom of conscience was a principle worth defending for everyone. Their legacy challenges us to do more than remember history. It challenges us to continue their work. Because religious liberty is not merely something we inherit. It is something we have a responsibility to preserve.

“Proclaim liberty throughout all the land unto all the inhabitants thereof.” — Leviticus 25:10
