Sabbath School Lesson Preview

When Love Drives the Ministry

Sabbath 8/29/2026

"What does ministry look like when love, not recognition, position, success, or personal preference, is what drives us?"

This week's Sabbath School lesson, “A Love-Driven Ministry,” takes us into Paul’s relationship with the church at Corinth. It is not a picture of easy ministry. Paul experienced suffering, disappointment, misunderstanding, and deep concern for the people he served. Yet his love for them remained steadfast because his ministry flowed from the love of Christ.

This lesson encourages us to look beyond what we do in ministry and examine how and why we do it. Paul exemplified compassion for those who were suffering, demonstrated dependence on God in overwhelming circumstances, upheld integrity when motives were questioned, and offered forgiveness when relationships were strained. A love-driven ministry seeks restoration instead of condemnation, allowing the character of Christ to shine through our relationships.

The Fragrance of Christ

Perhaps the most beautiful image in this lesson is Paul's description of believers as the "fragrance of Christ." Our ministry is not only about what we say from the pulpit, what we teach in a Sabbath School class, or what we achieve through church programs. Our attitudes, relationships, acts of forgiveness, compassion, and the way we treat others all communicate something about Jesus.

This week, take time to study the lesson prayerfully. Don't simply ask, “What can I learn?” Ask, “What is my life communicating about Christ to the people around me?”

Food for Thought:

"If your church family and community experienced Christ primarily through the way you treated them, what kind of 'fragrance' of Jesus would they encounter?"

Religious Liberty Corner

Freedom of Conscience Feature

Would You Defend Religious Liberty for Someone You Disagree With?

Rise Above Politics • Defend Universal Conscience • Prophetic Vigilance

Religious liberty sounds simple when our freedom is at stake. But what happens when the person asking for protection is from another denomination, another religion, another country, or holds views completely different from ours? That question is being tested right now.

A Current Religious Liberty Battle

On August 18, a federal appeals court upheld an order limiting immigration enforcement actions at eight houses of worship connected with Quaker, Sikh, and Cooperative Baptist Fellowship communities.

The religious groups had challenged a change in federal immigration-enforcement policy. They argued that the possibility of enforcement actions at their places of worship was discouraging immigrants from attending services and interfering with their ability to carry out their religious ministries.

The court concluded that the policy likely substantially burdened their religious exercise under the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (RFRA). The decision was narrow, but it raises an important question for Christians: How sacred is the freedom to worship?

Don’t Get Distracted by the Politics

Some will immediately view this issue through the lens of immigration. But for purposes of religious liberty, there is a deeper question. If people become afraid to enter a house of worship because of government action, and a church believes that action is interfering with its God-given responsibility to minister to people, when does that become a religious-liberty issue? We may disagree about immigration policy and still recognize the importance of that question. This is precisely why religious liberty must rise above partisan politics.

Jesus Didn’t Ask for a Passport

"I was a stranger, and ye took me in." — Matthew 25:35

The church’s responsibility to minister does not disappear because someone’s legal status, nationality, religion, or political beliefs make that ministry controversial. Neither does defending someone’s religious liberty mean that we endorse everything that person believes or does. It means we defend a principle: Government should not unnecessarily interfere with the free exercise of religion.

Adventists Should Understand This

Our own history should make Seventh-day Adventists particularly sensitive to this principle. There was a time when Sabbath-keeping Adventists were arrested, fined, and even imprisoned because their religious convictions conflicted with Sunday laws. Imagine if Christians in those days had responded: “That doesn’t affect me. I worship on Sunday.”

Today it may be a Sikh congregation. Tomorrow it may be a Baptist church. Another day it may be a Muslim community. And eventually, the person asking government to respect his or her conscience may be you—a Seventh-day Adventist trying to keep the seventh-day Sabbath.

Religious Liberty Cannot Be Selective

If we defend religious freedom only for Christians, we are not truly defending religious freedom. If we defend it only for people whose politics resemble ours, we are defending political allies—not liberty of conscience. Jesus gave us a principle that reaches beyond those who look, believe, or behave like us:

“Therefore all things whatsoever ye would that men should do to you, do ye even so to them.” — Matthew 7:12

There Is Also a Prophetic Reason

Revelation 13 warns of a future in which civil authority ultimately becomes involved in matters of worship and conscience. That is why Adventists have historically watched the use of governmental power in religious matters so carefully. God never gave civil government authority over the human conscience. True worship requires choice. “Choose you this day whom ye will serve.” (Joshua 24:15). You cannot force genuine worship. You cannot legislate genuine faith.

Religious Liberty Watch

When you hear about a religious-liberty controversy this week, ask instead: “What principle is at stake? Would I defend this principle if an Adventist were involved? A Muslim? Someone with opposite politics?” If our answer changes depending upon who needs the freedom, we may need to reconsider what religious liberty actually means.

Thought for the Week

"The true test of our commitment to religious liberty is not how strongly we defend our own freedom. It is whether we are willing to defend the freedom of conscience of someone with whom we profoundly disagree."

— Leviticus 25:10
